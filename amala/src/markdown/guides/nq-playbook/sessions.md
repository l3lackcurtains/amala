## The open is busier than the clock says

An 18-year minute-level study agrees across its history, discovery, and confirmation periods: Nasdaq futures activity roughly **triples over the half hour beginning 9:25 ET** — the market is waking before the bell, in the quote and pre-positioning, not at it. The 9:30 open minute itself prints ~3.25× the day's median one-minute range.

The practical consequence for funded risk: a stop that looks comfortable on the calm chart at 9:00 is being sized into a market about to run two to three times as active. Wicks at the open are normal market behavior, not anomalies to fade — size accordingly or wait for the first five minutes to resolve.

## Day-shape landmarks

- **10:00 ET** is the second spike — that is where the morning data releases land, right after the open's first hour of position-building.
- **11:00–13:30** is the acknowledged dead zone: thinning volume, false breaks, chop. Vendor and independent session guides converge here; the measured version is that midday spike-minutes are only barely larger than ordinary minutes.
- **15:50 ET is the session's second-biggest minute** — the market-on-close imbalance publication starts the close's repricing, and 15:59 finishes it. This is the last real directional window before the firm's auto-flat.
- **Power hour is a volatility window, not a directional one.** Across sixteen years, no 15-minute slot consistently drifts up or down once tested against coin-flip controls. One late-day continuation cell survived testing, and its own publisher labeled it "a candidate for a properly pre-registered test, not a finding." The regime warning that matters: a known close-window edge on ES reversed sign in the most recent sample.

## The overnight question

Globex opens at 6:00 PM ET with a burst — measured as the largest overnight event — then goes quiet by ~6:30. Education sources are broadly cautionary about thin overnight books, and the profitable-trader consensus from forums is NY-hours-only. A vocal fringe trades the 3:00 AM London leg on liquidity-trap behavior; there is no dataset making either case, so treat overnight trading as a specialty you must personally journal into, not a default.

## News anatomy

The measured shape of a Tier-1 release on NQ:

1. **The ten minutes before**: liquidity is pulled, volume dries. Nothing real trades here; spreads and slippage widen.
2. **The release minute**: 50–100 points in seconds is routine on a surprise, into a book that just thinned. The first minute is the widest, least informative bar of the day — entries "at the release" are entries into a gap.
3. **Minutes two through fifteen**: liquidity returns and the *second* move is what direction traders who trade news at all actually take. The first move reversed by the close on roughly a quarter to a third of releases depending on the event.

Release-type base rates from a trader-compiled event study: FOMC reverses its initial move roughly twice as often as CPI; GDP the least. Treat these as unaudited but reproducible counts, not official data.

## Firms legislate this clock

- **New NQ (mini) positions blocked around CPI, NFP, FOMC, and the presser** (a few minutes each side) at one major firm; micros capped during the window instead. Micros-only traders get a smaller version of the same rule, which is one more reason micros are the eval default.
- Another firm allows news trading outright but **prohibits two-sided straddle orders ahead of the release**.
- "Full news-event trading allowed" is marketed as a differentiator by at least one firm.

The dashboard rule: know your firm's number before your broker's calendar surprises you, and remember that a slippage overage into a daily loss limit counts against you exactly like a planned loss.

## Day-of-week: not settled

Weekday statistics conflict across sources — one long-run dataset ranks Thursday highest and Friday lowest for range; a live-data vendor ranks Friday among the largest NY-session ranges. Academic futures work confirms a weekday effect exists in *returns* but at magnitudes trivial next to NQ intraday noise; a circulating "majority of Mondays close green" count covers only a few years. There is no weekday filter robust enough to build risk rules on. Use session and news clocks; ignore Monday-brave trading.
