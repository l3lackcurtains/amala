Not trading rules — *game* rules. Each one is documented across firm disclosures, verified simulations, and paid-trader reports; each one converts an account killer into a designed non-event.

1. **Size from the floor, never from the target.** Risk 2–5% of the maximum loss limit per trade — on a $2,000 floor that is $40–100, which means micros on NQ until a cushion exists. The rule-verified Monte Carlos agree with the sizing guides and the forums: at a fixed positive edge, 0.5%-of-floor risk passes ~9 times out of 10 and 2%-of-balance sizing passes 1 in 10, even when "every dollar of drawdown looks fine on the balance sheet." The floor is the account; the first five trades in a row are the business plan.
2. **Set a personal daily stop strictly inside the firm's, and enforce it with a lockout.** If the firm's limit is −$2,000, stop at −$800–1,200; if there is no daily limit, that is a feature you must override, not exploit. Documented reasoning: the trades that end most accounts are the second and third of a day, taken to recover the first at bigger size; a platform lockout deletes the moment of decision. The firm that changed its DLL from "instant fail" to "lockout and survive" did so because enforcement measurably improved outcomes — borrow their research.
3. **Cap the best day below the consistency line before the day starts.** A 50%–55% consistency rule means your $1,900 trend-day hero *raises the target to $3,800* and converts the win into two weeks of extra grinding. The tactic traders run: banking partials at planned levels and ending strong days early — smooth equity is not a preference, it is compliance. If a firm's plan pays 100% of the first $25K, the math changes and lumpy is taxed differently — read the specific payout doc, not the subreddit.
4. **Match account structure to setup geometry, not to the promo.** Wide stops and round-trip risk need EOD-trailing or static floors. Fast-scalping closes tolerate intraday trails. News-reactive styles buy plans with generous news windows — and then *still* skip the first minute. The three drawdown types are three different games wearing one account label: the pass probability of a zero-edge trader changes ~10 points purely from the floor type. Choose the physics you can survive before choosing the fee you like.
5. **Know every clock before you're on it.** Minimum trading days, profitable-day counts that *reset after each payout*, loss allowances that *reset to zero* when money leaves the account, balance floors that must be above a number before a request opens, safety nets required for the first three claims, auto-flat at 4:10/4:45/4:59 depending on firm, half-session quirks that skip the flatten. The paid-trader habit: a one-page calendar per firm tracking days traded, cushion, gate status, and next eligible claim. The account dies when the gate is misunderstood, not when the market moves.
6. **Withdraw the moment you are eligible.** Published behavioral data (independent, 300K+ accounts) plus the barrier-model simulation converge: leaving profits in a funded account to "compound" roughly doubles the odds the run ends dead; withdrawn profits are permanently yours while in-account profits are the next drawdown's collateral. A $500 claim is not a small win — it is the entire product the model sells. Small and frequent also sits inside the documented payout-audit tolerance that large claims do not.
7. **Scale sideways, never vertical.** Five accounts running the same proven 1-MNQ plan at identical risk is five payout gates and five chances; it is not five trades. The rulebook tolerates it (same direction only — reverse copying across accounts is hedging and closes accounts; copy ratios ≥ 1.0 or followers orphan; household caps are real — up to 20 funded accounts at one major futures firm). What it is not: a fix for an unproven edge — the datasets call persistence through re-purchases "feeding the firm's model," so the first two accounts stay one strategy at one risk unit.
8. **Buy options after proof, not before.** The fee schedule is the tax on practice: average lifetime fee spend is ~$800 over ~3 challenges, and the industry funnel pays roughly one in seven buyers. The hack is sequencing — sim and one small account until a journal shows positive expectancy on one setup, then a real eval, then lateral farm. Budget $1,500–3,000 of working capital as a business line (evals, fees, a payout-gate's worth of drawdown), keep a second income, and treat every pre-proof eval purchase as tuition at the vendor's exchange rate.
9. **One instrument, one strategy, one playbook — until the payout history is boring.** Switching is the documented hidden cost: every instrument swap resets the structural memory that makes execution cheap (stops "just know where they go"), and the sample-per-setup math halves with each added symbol. The consistent earners' expansion pattern in the records: narrow until money, then *one* new book at a time, correlation-checked against the firm's hedging rules.
10. **Re-measure the tape monthly; yesterday's parameters are a risk position.** The clearest number in all the research: the same instrument's gap-fill behavior measured 63% on one window and 38% six months later; a stop size that fit one month failed badly the next; one year's "normal" daily range became the next year's small day. Data platforms that traders actually use publish exactly this drift (the point of the report is that it updates, not that it flatters you). The eval-time habit: before sizing a new month, check current range, break, and fill statistics and re-pick the stop distance — plan or don't plan, but measure.
11. **Keep a written no-trade list and read it before the open.** Tier-1 windows (the release minute is the widest, least-informative bar of the day, and firms block entries around them anyway), your own tilt conditions (third loss, chop hours, boredom), and the calendar entries the community's best operators encode: compressed Mondays for aggressive plans, half-day session closes, NFP week midweeks. The documented cure for most eval deaths is *absence* from the exact conditions that produce them.
12. **Grade the day on rule-following, not P&L — and treat a profitable rule-break as a loss.** Every institution's responsible-trading text, every psychology survey that survives, and the simulators' "failures die at the floor" finding all describe the same mechanism: outcomes reward the behaviors that destroy accounts (oversize, hold losers, go full port) until one settles the debt. The ledger to keep alongside the P&L is rule compliance; the day the rule-break *wins* is the day the next three weeks become fragile.
