The honest case for props, and the honest size of it. Every number below carries its denominator — that's not hedging, it's the difference between a business plan and a screenshot.

## What the funnel actually publishes

One futures firm states all four numbers openly (its disclosures are the industry's best public funnel): ~17% of *combines* initiated complete the eval; ~52% of *people* who try eventually reach funded status (persistence is part of the product); about a third of funded accounts ever receive a payout; **0.71% reach the live-capital stage.** A forex/futures dataset spanning 300,000+ accounts across firms lands in the same shape: ~14% funded, of those ~45% paid once — **~7% of challenge buyers ever get paid** — at an average of ~$800 in fees over roughly three attempts. Another tracked firm reports 3.7% of buyers paid.

Read these as a business model, not a verdict on you: pass rates quoted per-attempt and per-person differ by ~3×, payout *counts* are not payout *recipients*, and no firm publishes days-to-pass distributions or second-payout survival — those gaps are why the simulations below matter.

## Why the option is worth buying

| Argument | The numbers | Source class |
|---|---|---|
| Leverage without capital | Personal futures margin for size ≈ 10K+; a 50K sim's "capital" is a $2K floor bought for $35–150/mo promo pricing | Exchange + vendor fee sheets |
| Fees are the whole downside of a *single* attempt | $35–150 per eval vs $2,000 of real account risk avoided | Firm pricing |
| Pass odds respond violently to sizing | Same positive edge: ~93% pass at 0.5% risk vs ~9% at 2% (10K-run sim); rules-verified simulator shows 38%→96% across its risk profiles | Simulations, disclosed assumptions |
| The payout gate is the real filter | One rules-verified Monte Carlo: 71% of funded runs pass the eval math, only 40% ever collect five qualifying winning days | Simulation |
| Withdrawal dominates reinvestment | Barrier model: reinvesting every payout roughly *doubles* modeled ruin vs claiming; funded cushion thins with every claim on trailing floors | Working paper + sim |
| Typical claims are small and regular | A major firm's public ledger on an average day is dominated by $500–$2,000 payouts; the big dataset's mean payout is ~4% of account size | Company ledger; FPFX data |
| Payouts are taxed like work, not trading | Schedule C + ~15.3% self-employment (the contract sits in the firm's account, not yours); 1099 thresholds and offshore no-forms mean *you* own the reporting | Professional guidance |
| Working-capital sizing is itself the model | The barrier paper's optimum for a multi-account operation: ~$1,500–3,000 of eval budget; below $1K most simulated runs die from fees alone | Working paper |

The structural reframe that makes this high-ROI *and* boring: you are not trading a $50K account. You are paying a small option premium on an edge you have to prove elsewhere, and the payout schedule you designed before buying the eval is the product. The consistent-earner model the trader reports describe is $2K–$4K months built from daily $500–$2,000 claims across a few accounts — not the $10K screenshots, whose industry equivalent is the firm's own revenue line: at one major CFD prop, annual revenue (~$422M) *exceeds* what it paid out across all customers. The model pays traders who are boring on purpose.

## Time — the number no firm publishes, and the three that matter

1. **To first payout:** floors set by rules (min days, buffer builds, qualifying-day counts, request windows) mean the fastest realistic path is ~6–12 weeks *if skilled*, 4–12 months *if typical*, counting two-to-three failed evals.
2. **To consistency:** the most-upvoted sentence in the trader-report corpus, from people who got there: "18 months to 3 years" — with the community's standing warning that **the first 2–4 funded accounts usually die** before any payout cadence exists.
3. **Per attempt:** slow is allowed and costs only time plus the subscription — the fee schedule punishes grinding small longer than it punishes disciplined risk. The same fee schedule makes aggressive sizing the expensive shortcut it looks like: 83% of simulated per-attempt failures die at the floor, not the target.
