Discipline content usually reads like character advice. This page reads like a diagnostics table, because the evidence class here is unusually good: two decades of peer-reviewed behavioral finance, one 300K+ prop-account dataset, one regulatory-records futures study, two rule-verified simulators, and the firms' own rule text. They all converge on the same few mechanisms.

## What the academics measured (and why it transfers to evals)

- **Turnover is the tax.** The foundational 66,465-household study: the most-active quintile earned 11.4% a year while the market earned 17.9%, and the same traders' *chosen* stocks underperformed the ones they merely held. Overtrading is not a vibe — it's a measured cost channel with a commission line, a bid-ask line, and an adverse-selection line. A prop eval is turnover taxed twice: fees, and the floor.
- **Persistence is not skill.** The most relevant single paper in all of this: Brazilian regulatory records of ~20,000 people who started day-trading mini index futures. About four in five quit within the first year; of those still at it past 300 days, 97% lost money net of fees — the headline framing being that only the top few percent earned more than a doorman's wage, and the single best survivor's "salary" came with a daily standard deviation larger than most people's monthly rent. The study's most quoted conclusion is the one this library keeps meeting: **there was no evidence that experience improved outcomes — the traders who stayed did not get better.** For prop traders it reads as a warning about the eval-purchase flywheel itself: 19 combines in a year is persistence, and the dataset says persistence alone buys nothing except vendor revenue.
- **Pain threshold, not P&L threshold.** The behavioral mechanism behind "you can know the rules and still break them": investors realize winners ~2.5× more readily than losers, and the *held* losers subsequently outperform the sold winners. Translated to a trailing-floor account: the trade you "can't stop out of" is the one that defines your day, and stop placement that respects your *pain* tolerance instead of the structure guarantees the floor eventually arrives before the stop does. The fix is not courage; it is placing the stop before the position exists and sizing so the structure stop is inside your pain threshold (risk-first sizing is literally the mechanism the disposition effect says you cannot improvise under load).
- **Even pros rarely beat wages.** The Taiwanese study of 1,383 dedicated day traders: 1,365 lost. The right reading is not "trading doesn't work" — it's that *the distribution is brutally skewed and the survivor tail is where all the visible income lives*, which is exactly what the prop funnels show from the other direction: the marketing is the tail, the fee model is the median.

## What the prop datasets add

- **The second and third trades are the killers.** In the 300K+ account analysis (the one behind the "7% ever paid" funnel): the trade that ends a run is usually *not* the first loss of the day — it is the following trades, taken **to recover the first, sized bigger than the plan**. And the same psychological problem survives passing: a trader who tightened up for three weeks and passed can drift straight back into pre-pass behavior funded, because "funded" didn't change the behavior that nearly ended the challenge. This is the eval game's central behavioral fact — the market rarely kills you, your *reaction* to the market does, on a schedule.
- **Simulations: risk is the variable that answers to itself.** The rule-verified Monte Carlo of a $50K combine found **every single simulated failure died at the trailing loss limit** — no "ran out of time," no "missed the target." Failure is mechanically a risk event wearing a market disguise. And the community's 10,000-run FTMO simulation with a *known positive edge*: 93% pass at 0.5% risk/trade, 9% at 2%. Same strategy, same win rate, tenfold difference in survival — the most direct quantification in existence of "discipline pays."
- **The patience price schedule.** A zero-edge simulation battery by daily swing size: $100 swings → 14% pass odds over a 189-day median; $300 → 27% over 49; $800 → ~10 days and a ruin explosion. Trading small on a no-deadline product "costs time, not much else" — with the monthly subscription as the explicit tax — while trading big costs the 83%. The rational plan sits in between and looks, on paper, exactly like this dashboard's resident style: $200–400 planned days, micro units, patience as a budget.
- **The payout gate is where discipline actually dies.** Same simulator's funded-stage funnel: a high fraction of accounts reach funded, but far fewer ever *collect* five qualifying days. The community's own summary of the funded grind: first payout at 9 months; 18 months to 3 years to consistency; **2–4 funded accounts usually blow before one pays.** The psychological literature the independent writeups point at explains why: house-money sizing after withdrawals, audit-fear hoarding, and the recovery-trade reflex meeting its biggest trigger — losing real, claimable money instead of virtual.

## The firms' own confessions

The rule text of a major futures prop is effectively a behavioral diagnosis list — maximum position on most trades, letting losers run bigger than winners, trading without stops, "going full port, tilt, FOMO, or revenge," and disrespecting the daily limit. What's notable is the enforcement change the firms *made*: one moved the daily loss limit from instant account-failure to a one-day lockout, on the explicit research framing that lockouts build the discipline the old fail-everything model punished into gambling. Reset purchase limits ("2 per day, reinforces responsible trading") say the same thing: the platform's own data caught the pattern of trade-reset-trade-reset as a tilt signature, and the vendor of the game now patches the player against himself mid-session. The lesson for your plan is cheap to run and free to adopt: **use the lockout, buy the reset only as a last resort, and treat the firm's enforcement features as the documented best version of what self-control is worth.**

## The one-sentence synthesis

Every serious number in every source class says the same thing: expectancy lives in the *distribution of your losses* — their size, their sequence, and your reaction speed — and the market is merely the thing that samples from it.
